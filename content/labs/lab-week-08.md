## Probability Theory and Probabilistic Models Lab

This lab works through three probabilistic tools in turn, each applied to behavioral data.

**Part 1** takes the Poisson process from a rate estimate to a tested model. You will estimate a response rate by maximum likelihood, attach a standard error and confidence interval, and then run the three checks the model makes available: whether the variance of per-interval counts equals their mean, whether observed inter-response times follow the predicted exponential, and whether a quantile-quantile plot falls on the diagonal. Two 50-minute records are supplied that return the same rate estimate with overlapping confidence intervals. One was generated by a Poisson process and one by bouts of fast responding separated by pauses. Nothing in the point estimate distinguishes them; every diagnostic does. The exercise makes concrete a claim from the chapter: a fitted parameter reported without the checks that could have rejected the model is a claim that the model was never tested.

**Part 2** applies Bayesian updating to functional assessment. Starting from a uniform prior over four candidate functions (attention, escape, tangible, automatic), you will specify a Poisson likelihood encoding what each function predicts for each condition, then update after every session and track how the posterior moves. Two datasets are supplied. In the clear case the posterior resolves within two sessions, and the sequence is instructive: a single elevated attention session leaves attention and automatic tied at 0.50 each, because both predicted elevation there, and the tie is broken by a condition in which responding was *absent*. In the ambiguous case the evidence per session is small, the posterior climbs gradually and non-monotonically, and a sensitivity analysis shows the final confidence depends on assumptions that the clear case was immune to.

**Part 3** uses a bootstrap Monte Carlo procedure to put a confidence interval on a behavioral parameter without an analytic formula, and compares it against the parametric interval on the same data.

Together the three parts illustrate what a probabilistic model buys you over a deterministic one: it commits to a distribution rather than a value, which is what allows data to contradict it, and it reports uncertainty as a quantity rather than leaving it implicit.
