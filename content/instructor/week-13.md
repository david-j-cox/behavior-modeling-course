## Lecture Objectives

By the end of this session, students should be able to:

- Present a complete modeling project structured around the 8-step framework, demonstrating synthesis of the course material
- Defend their modeling choices---phenomenon selection, scope, functional form, assumptions, and evaluation criteria---under questioning from peers and the instructor
- Critically evaluate a peer's modeling project, identifying strengths, limitations, and potential extensions
- Articulate what the modeling process revealed about their chosen behavioral phenomenon that verbal description alone could not
- Reflect on their own development as modelers across the thirteen-week arc of the course

## Suggested Session Structure

**Estimated duration: 3 hours (with a break)**

This is presentation day. The structure is different from every other week in the course. There is no new content to deliver. Your role shifts from lecturer to evaluator, facilitator, and timekeeper.

### Before the Session

- Confirm the presentation order. If students submitted preferences, honor them where possible. Otherwise, randomize the order and post it before class so students can prepare mentally.
- Print or have ready the evaluation rubric (see Evaluation Criteria below) with one copy per student presenter. You will complete these during or immediately after each presentation.
- Prepare a brief opening and closing (see below). The rest of the session belongs to the students.
- Have a timer visible to both you and the presenter. Time management is critical when you have multiple presentations.

### Opening (5--10 min)

Set the tone. Remind students that this is a professional presentation, not a casual discussion. The expectations:

- Each presenter has **15--20 minutes** for their presentation.
- Each presentation is followed by **5--10 minutes** of Q&A from peers and the instructor.
- Audience members are expected to ask at least one question during the session. This is not optional---it is part of professional development.
- Feedback should be constructive: identify what was done well and what could be strengthened.

Briefly acknowledge the scope of what they have accomplished. They began Week 1 not knowing what a formal model was. They are now presenting original modeling work. That is a significant intellectual achievement.

### Presentations (variable, depending on class size)

For a class of 6--8 students, expect presentations to take approximately 2--2.5 hours including Q&A.

**During each presentation:**

- Take notes on the rubric. Mark which steps of the 8-step framework are addressed clearly, which are addressed but could be stronger, and which are missing or unclear.
- Note specific strengths to mention in your feedback. Students remember specific praise ("Your dimensional analysis was particularly clear") more than general praise ("Good job").
- Note specific areas for improvement, framed as questions or suggestions rather than criticisms ("What would happen to your model if you relaxed the steady-state assumption?").
- Track time. Give a 5-minute warning and a 1-minute warning. If a student runs over, allow them to finish their current point and then move to Q&A.

**During Q&A:**

- Let peers ask first. Jump in only if the Q&A stalls or if there is a critical point that no one has raised.
- Use the discussion prompts below to seed questions if needed.
- If a student's question reveals a common misunderstanding, briefly address it for the whole class.

**After each presentation (1--2 min):**

- Provide brief verbal feedback. Highlight one strength and one area for development. Save detailed feedback for the written rubric.
- Thank the presenter and transition to the next one.

### Break (10--15 min)

Place the break roughly at the midpoint of the presentations. Students need a mental reset, especially if the presentations are dense.

### Closing the Course (10--15 min)

After the final presentation and Q&A, take 10--15 minutes to close out the course. See the Closing the Course section below.

## Discussion Prompts

Use these prompts during Q&A if the conversation needs direction. They are designed to probe the modeling decisions that matter most.

1. **"What assumption in this model would you most want to relax, and what would happen if you did?"** This tests whether the presenter understands their own assumptions and their consequences. A strong answer identifies a specific assumption, explains why it might be wrong, and describes qualitatively how the model's behavior would change.

2. **"How would you test this model with new data?"** This probes the difference between fitting and predicting. A strong answer describes what kind of data would constitute a genuine test---not the same dataset, but a new dataset from different participants, different conditions, or a different context.

3. **"If you added one more parameter to your model, what would it represent?"** This tests whether the presenter has thought about model extensions and whether they understand the tradeoff between complexity and parsimony. A strong answer identifies a specific behavioral process that the current model ignores and explains why capturing it would (or would not) be worth the added complexity.

4. **"How does your model connect to models we covered earlier in the course?"** This tests synthesis. Can the presenter see the relationship between their project and the broader course content? For example, does a discounting model relate to demand analysis? Does a computational model implement a process that the Rescorla-Wagner model describes algebraically?

5. **"What was the most surprising thing your model told you?"** This is a softer question, useful for presenters who seem nervous or when you want to shift the tone. It invites the presenter to reflect on the modeling process, not just the product.

6. **"You mentioned [specific limitation]. How serious is that limitation? Could it change your conclusions?"** This pushes the presenter to evaluate their own limitations honestly. The goal is not to undermine their work but to gauge the depth of their understanding.

7. **"If a colleague in your lab wanted to use your model for a practical decision, what would you tell them about its reliability?"** This connects modeling to application and tests whether the presenter can communicate uncertainty to a non-technical audience.

## Evaluation Criteria

The evaluation rubric is organized around the 8-step framework. Each step is rated on a three-point scale: **Clear** (the step is addressed explicitly and competently), **Present but Underdeveloped** (the step is mentioned but not fully worked through), or **Missing/Unclear** (the step is absent or confused).

### Step 1: Phenomenon

- Is the behavioral phenomenon clearly stated?
- Would a listener unfamiliar with the topic understand what is being modeled?
- Is the phenomenon grounded in behavioral science?

### Step 2: Scope

- Are the boundaries of the model defined?
- Is it clear what the model does and does not cover?
- Is the scope appropriately narrow for a course project?

### Step 3: Principles

- Are the relevant behavioral principles or quantitative laws identified?
- Is there a clear connection between the principles and the chosen model form?

### Step 4: Assumptions

- Are all simplifying assumptions stated explicitly?
- Does the presenter demonstrate awareness of which assumptions are most fragile?
- Are the assumptions reasonable given the scope?

### Step 5: Formulation

- Is the model presented both verbally and mathematically?
- Are all symbols and parameters defined?
- Can the audience follow the logic from verbal description to equation (or algorithm)?

### Step 6: Dimensional Consistency

- Is dimensional consistency verified?
- Are the units of every term accounted for?

### Step 7: Constraints and Initial Conditions

- Are parameter constraints specified (e.g., positive values, bounded ranges)?
- Are starting values or initial conditions stated where applicable?
- Are the conditions under which the model applies made clear?

### Step 8: Evaluation and Validation

- Is the model tested against data (real or simulated)?
- Are results presented clearly, with appropriate figures and tables?
- Are parameter estimates reported with measures of uncertainty?
- If models are compared, is the comparison principled (AIC, BIC, cross-validation)?
- Are predictions derived and checked for behavioral plausibility?

### Additional Criteria

Beyond the 8-step framework, consider:

- **Presentation quality.** Is the presentation organized, clear, and within the time limit? Are figures legible and well-labeled?
- **Engagement with questions.** Does the presenter respond thoughtfully to Q&A? Can they defend their choices and acknowledge weaknesses?
- **Connection to theory.** Does the presenter connect their model back to behavioral theory? Is the model more than a curve fit?
- **Intellectual honesty.** Does the presenter acknowledge limitations, uncertainties, and open questions?

## Closing the Course

After the last presentation, take a few minutes to close the course intentionally. This matters more than you might think---students remember endings.

**Acknowledge the achievement.** Be specific. In Week 1, most students could not write a mathematical model from memory. They can now build models, fit them to data, compare alternatives, check dimensional consistency, and present their work. That is a meaningful change, and it is worth saying so.

**Revisit the arc.** Briefly trace the course trajectory: from the introduction to modeling (Week 1), through the classical models (Weeks 2--5), to model comparison (Week 6), model construction (Week 7), probabilistic and multilevel models (Weeks 8--9), dynamical systems (Week 10), computational models (Week 11), machine learning (Week 12), and now to original work (Week 13). The arc is from consumer to producer. They began by reading models; they end by building them.

**Name what stays.** The specific models will fade from memory. The 8-step framework, the habit of stating assumptions, the instinct to check dimensions, the practice of comparing alternatives---these are the durable skills. Tell them so.

**Invite forward-looking reflection.** Where will they use these skills next? In their own research? In reading the literature? In applied practice? The modeling framework is not specific to this course---it is a general tool for quantitative thinking.

**Thank them.** Teaching a modeling course to behavior scientists is a particular kind of challenge. The students who make it to Week 13 have done genuinely difficult intellectual work. A sincere thank-you is appropriate.
