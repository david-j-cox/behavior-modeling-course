## Why Model Behavior?

Behavior science aspires to be a natural science of behavior. Natural sciences make quantitative predictions through models -- formal representations of how processes work that can be tested against observation. The experimental analysis of behavior has produced impressive quantitative regularities (the matching law, delay discounting functions, demand curves), but the broader discipline often treats these as isolated findings rather than as instances of a general modeling enterprise. This course introduces that enterprise.

Models matter because they force clarity. A verbal claim like "reinforcement strengthens behavior" is useful, but it does not tell you by how much, under what conditions, or how the effect changes when parameters change. A mathematical model must answer these questions or fail visibly. Models also matter because they bridge basic findings and application. If you want to predict how a token economy will perform, or how changing the price of a commodity will alter consumption, you need a quantitative framework.

## What Students Will Gain

By the end of this course, students should be able to:

1. **Read and evaluate quantitative models** encountered in the behavior-science literature, understanding not only what a model predicts but why it is structured the way it is and what assumptions it requires.

2. **Apply the 8-step modeling framework** to any behavioral phenomenon, producing a clearly specified, dimensionally consistent, and empirically testable model.

3. **Fit models to data** using standard estimation procedures (least squares, maximum likelihood, Bayesian methods) and interpret the resulting parameter estimates in behavioral terms.

4. **Compare competing models** using principled criteria (information criteria, cross-validation, qualitative predictions) rather than relying solely on goodness-of-fit.

5. **Build simple computational models** including simulations and agent-based models, and use them to explore behavioral dynamics that resist closed-form mathematical treatment.

6. **Communicate modeling work** clearly, translating between equations and plain language.

7. **Identify the limitations of any model**, including their own, and articulate what additional data or model extensions would be needed to address those limitations.
